## 2. Abstract

This project evaluated whether transformer models fine-tuned on modest amounts of data can
distinguish machine-generated text from human-written text, and whether they improve on the
classical baselines established at midterm. Two datasets were used: the DAIGT V2 corpus of
student essays and the HC3 corpus of question-answer pairs. Both were balanced to three thousand
documents per class and split identically to the midterm experiments, so that classical and
transformer results are directly comparable on the same twelve hundred test documents. BERT and
DeBERTa were each fine-tuned across eight hyperparameter configurations per dataset, giving
thirty-two runs, and their predictions were combined by a validation-weighted soft vote. DeBERTa
achieved the strongest results on both datasets, reaching a weighted F1 of 0.9933 on DAIGT and
0.9992 on HC3, the latter representing a single misclassification in twelve hundred documents.
BERT improved substantially on the classical baselines for HC3 but proved unstable, varying by
0.0267 F1 across random seeds on DAIGT and failing to reproduce its recorded score when
retrained. The ensemble did not outperform the stronger individual model, a negative result whose
mechanism is examined in detail. Additional analyses quantified the effect of sequence truncation,
duplicate contamination, and seed variance on the reported figures.

---

## 3. Dataset Description

Two publicly available datasets were used, each posing the same binary problem of separating
human-written from machine-generated text in a different domain.

**Dataset 1, DAIGT V2 Train Dataset**, distributed through Kaggle under the MIT licence, contains
44,868 student-style argumentative essays labelled as either human-written or generated by a large
language model. The human portion is drawn predominantly from the PERSUADE corpus of student
writing, while the machine portion aggregates output from seventeen distinct generators including
GPT-family models, Llama 2, Mistral, Falcon, Claude, and PaLM. The native class distribution is
27,371 human documents to 17,497 machine-generated documents. Documents are long, averaging 2,216
characters.

**Dataset 2, HC3, the Human ChatGPT Comparison Corpus**, distributed through HuggingFace under the
CC-BY-SA-4.0 licence and introduced by Guo and colleagues in arXiv preprint 2301.07597, contains
24,322 questions each paired with one or more human answers and one or more ChatGPT answers, drawn
from Reddit ELI5, finance, medicine, open-domain question answering, and Wikipedia computer science
sources. The corpus provides no label column directly; labels were constructed by expanding the two
answer lists, assigning label zero to human answers and label one to ChatGPT answers, which yields
85,449 labelled documents split 58,546 to 26,903. Answers are considerably shorter than DAIGT
essays. One detail worth recording is that the identifier field in HC3 is named `index` rather than
`id`, contrary to what was stated in the midterm report.

Both datasets were reduced to a balanced sample of three thousand documents per class, giving six
thousand documents each. Balancing used a fixed random seed of 42 so that the sample is exactly
reproducible and, more importantly, exactly matches the sample used at midterm.

## 4. Project Implementation Detail

### Task 1: Reconstructing the midterm split and verifying comparability

**Description.** The final deliverable places Naïve Bayes, Logistic Regression, and Support Vector
Machine results beside BERT and DeBERTa results in a single table. That comparison is only
meaningful if every model was evaluated on identical test documents. The midterm split was
therefore rebuilt from first principles rather than assumed, and its correctness was verified
before any transformer was trained.

**Code.** The midterm balancing function and text-cleaning pipeline were reproduced exactly, after
which the data was split in two independent ways and the two results compared.

```python
def balance(df, n, seed=42):
    parts = []
    for value in sorted(df['label'].unique()):
        subset = df[df['label'] == value]
        parts.append(subset.sample(n=min(n, len(subset)), random_state=seed))
    return pd.concat(parts).sample(frac=1, random_state=seed).reset_index(drop=True)

Xtr_lit, Xte_lit, ytr_lit, yte_lit = train_test_split(
    df['clean_text'], df['label'], test_size=0.2, random_state=42, stratify=df['label'])
idx_tr, idx_te, _, _ = train_test_split(
    df.index.values, df['label'].values, test_size=0.2, random_state=42,
    stratify=df['label'].values)

assert np.array_equal(np.asarray(Xtr_lit.index), idx_tr)
assert np.array_equal(np.asarray(Xte_lit.index), idx_te)
```

**Sample output.**

```
D1  Naive Bayes            BoW     (0.9567, 0.958, 0.9567, 0.9566)  MATCH
D1  Support Vector Machine TF-IDF  (0.9875, 0.9877, 0.9875, 0.9875) MATCH
D2  Logistic Regression    BoW     (0.9333, 0.9346, 0.9333, 0.9333) MATCH
...
VERIFIED: all 24 midterm numbers reproduced, split is identical to the midterm split
```

**Code description.** Because scikit-learn's partition depends only on the random state, the
stratification array, and the number of samples, splitting the dataframe index produces exactly the
same partition as splitting a text column. Index-based splitting therefore recovers the midterm
partition while leaving both the raw text and the cleaned text accessible, which matters because the
two model families require different inputs. The assertions confirm order-sensitive equality. As a
stronger check, all six classical models were refitted and all twenty-four midterm metrics were
reproduced to four decimal places, which verifies the entire data pipeline rather than the split
alone. A validation set of 480 documents was then carved from the 4,800 training documents,
producing a final partition of 4,320 training, 480 validation, and 1,200 test documents per dataset,
with the test set containing exactly 600 documents of each class.

### Task 2: Text representation and sequence-length diagnostics

**Description.** The classical models consume the aggressively cleaned text produced at midterm,
which lowercases, removes every non-alphabetic character, discards stopwords, and lemmatises. That
pipeline was deliberately not applied to the transformers.

**Code.**

```python
def normalise(t):
    return re.sub(r'\s+', ' ', str(t)).strip()

ds = ds.map(lambda b: tok(b['text'], truncation=True, max_length=128), batched=True)
```

**Sample output.**

| Dataset | Median tokens | Mean | 95th percentile | Truncated | Median share retained |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| DAIGT | 409 | 439.8 | 803 | 99.6% | 31.3% |
| HC3 | 165 | 181.9 | 376 | 61.1% | 77.6% |

**Code description.** Punctuation rhythm, capitalisation, and function-word usage are among the most
discriminative signals for identifying machine-generated text, and the midterm cleaning removes all
three. Applying it to a transformer would discard precisely the information the model is designed to
exploit, so the transformers received raw text with whitespace normalisation only. The two model
families therefore see different inputs, each appropriate to its class, and readers comparing rows
of the final table should be aware of this. The diagnostic table quantifies the cost of the
specified maximum sequence length of 128 tokens: on DAIGT, 99.6 percent of essays exceed the limit
and the median essay contributes only 31.3 percent of its tokens to the model. This is the single
largest limitation of the study and is discussed in Section 6.

### Task 3: The fine-tuning harness

**Description.** Thirty-two fine-tuning runs were required on a desktop GPU that also drives the
display, so the harness was built to survive interruption without losing completed work.

**Code.**

```python
def run_one(tag, model_key, lr, bs, wd, seed=42, epochs=5, max_len=128, verbose=True):

    resume = find_checkpoint(CKPT_DIR / key)
    ...
    trainer.train(resume_from_checkpoint=resume)
```

**Sample output.**

```
[done] D2_DeBERTa_lr3e-05_bs16_wd0.1_s42  val_f1=0.9937 test_f1=0.9992  4.2 min  peak 4.22 GiB
[skip] D1_BERT_lr3e-05_bs32_wd0.1_s42     test_f1=0.9908
```

**Code description.** Three independent mechanisms provide resilience. A completed run writes a
metrics file and a probability file, and on re-entry a run possessing both returns immediately
without touching the GPU, which is why re-executing the notebook takes minutes rather than hours.
Within a run, the trainer checkpoints at the end of every epoch, so a process killed mid-run resumes
from its last completed epoch with optimizer state and early-stopping counters intact. All result
files are written to a temporary path and moved into place atomically, so a process killed during a
write cannot leave a partial file that a later pass would mistake for a completed run. The
checkpoint directory is deleted only after the metrics file has landed, in that order. This design
was tested by an actual power cycle, after which all forty runs and every derived artefact were
found intact with no partial files.

Training used AdamW with a linear schedule, a warmup ratio of 0.1, a maximum of five epochs with
early stopping on validation weighted F1 after two epochs without improvement, dropout of 0.1 as
provided by both checkpoints, and bfloat16 mixed precision. Bfloat16 rather than float16 was
required because DeBERTa overflows in float16; the RTX 3060 Ti supports bfloat16 natively.

### Task 4 and Task 5: The hyperparameter sweep

**Description.** Each model was fine-tuned under the eight configurations specified, formed by
crossing learning rates of 2e-5 and 3e-5, batch sizes of 16 and 32, and weight decays of 0.01 and
0.1, on each of the two datasets.

**Sample output.**

```
--- run 9/32  D1 DeBERTa lr=3e-05 bs=16 wd=0.01 ---
[done] D1_DeBERTa_lr3e-05_bs16_wd0.01_s42  val_f1=0.9896 test_f1=0.9933  3.9 min  peak 4.22 GiB
```

**Code description.** BERT is `bert-base-uncased` with 110 million parameters. The BERT variation is
DeBERTa, specifically the `microsoft/deberta-v3-base` checkpoint, with 184 million parameters. Both
share an identical backbone of twelve layers, 768 hidden units, and twelve attention heads, so the
comparison isolates architectural and pretraining differences rather than model capacity. DeBERTa
differs in three respects: it uses disentangled attention, representing each token by separate
content and position vectors and computing content-to-position and position-to-content attention
terms rather than summing position into content as BERT does; it uses a 128,100-token SentencePiece
vocabulary against BERT's 30,522-token WordPiece vocabulary, which accounts for nearly all of the
parameter difference; and it was pretrained with replaced-token detection rather than masked
language modelling, giving a learning signal at every token position instead of only the masked
fraction.

Across the thirty-two runs, early stopping triggered on ten. BERT averaged 125.8 seconds per run at
a peak of 2.96 GiB of GPU memory, DeBERTa 212.7 seconds at a peak of 6.18 GiB. Total training time
for all forty runs, including the seed-robustness runs described in Task 7, was 1.91 hours.

### Task 6: The ensemble

**Description.** The two model families were combined by weighted soft voting, averaging predicted
class probabilities rather than voting on predicted labels.

**Code.**

```python
ws = np.arange(0, 1.0001, 0.05)
val_f1 = [weighted_metrics(pb['val_labels'],
          (w*pb['val_probs'] + (1-w)*pd_['val_probs']).argmax(1))[3] for w in ws]
best_w = float(ws[int(np.argmax(val_f1))])
test_mix = best_w*pb['test_probs'] + (1-best_w)*pd_['test_probs']
```

**Sample output.**

```
D1  members BERT=0.9908 DeBERTa=0.9933 -> ensemble test F1=0.9925  (w=0.35)
D2  members BERT=0.9875 DeBERTa=0.9992 -> ensemble test F1=0.9992  (w=0.00)
```

**Code description.** The member configurations were selected by validation F1 and the mixing weight
was tuned on validation only; the test set was used exactly once, after both choices were frozen.
Hard majority voting was not available because two members cannot break a tie. The ensemble did not
improve on the stronger member, which is analysed in Section 6.

### Task 7: Seed robustness

**Description.** The sweep uses a single random seed, and configurations separated by fractions of a
percent may differ only by noise. The winning configuration for each model and dataset was therefore
repeated at two further seeds.

### Task 8: Duplicate and contamination audit

**Description.** Because HC3 is constructed by expanding lists of answers, the same answer text can
appear more than once. If a duplicated document falls on both sides of the split, test performance
is inflated.

**Sample output.**

| Dataset | Test size | Leaked rows | Percent of test | Largest F1 change |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DAIGT | 1200 | 0 | 0.00% | 0.0000 |
| HC3 | 1200 | 8 | 0.67% | 0.0010 |

**Code description.** Documents were hashed after normalisation and test documents whose text also
appears in training or validation were identified. DAIGT is clean. HC3 contains eight affected test
documents, several of which are not answers at all but API failure messages such as "Too many

scored and altering it would invalidate the combined table. Instead every model was rescored on the
remaining clean subset. The largest change to any model's weighted F1 was 0.0010 and no ranking
changed, so the contamination does not affect the study's conclusions.

## 6. Discussion

**Transformers improve most where the classical baseline is weakest.** The gain over the best
classical model was 0.0058 F1 on DAIGT and 0.0659 on HC3. This pattern is consistent with the
nature of the two tasks. Essays in DAIGT differ from human writing in ways that surface-level word
frequency statistics already capture well, so a linear model over TF-IDF features performs close to
ceiling. Short conversational answers in HC3 differ in stylistic and structural ways that require
contextual representation, which is where the transformer architecture contributes.

**The DeBERTa advantage is consistent, and the BERT advantage is not.** DeBERTa outperformed BERT
in both datasets and was stable across seeds, varying by 0.0033 F1 on DAIGT and 0.0025 on HC3. BERT
varied by 0.0267 on DAIGT. This matters for interpreting Table 2: the BERT figure of 0.9908 on
DAIGT exceeds the Support Vector Machine baseline of 0.9875, but the mean across three seeds is
0.9800, which is below that baseline. The honest conclusion is therefore narrower than Table 2
alone suggests. DeBERTa outperforms every classical baseline on both datasets, while BERT's
advantage over the Support Vector Machine on DAIGT does not survive variation in the random seed.

A related observation reinforces this. When the winning configurations were retrained with
identical seed, data, and hyperparameters in order to save model weights, DeBERTa reproduced its
recorded test F1 exactly on both datasets, while BERT did not, differing by 0.0058 on DAIGT and
0.0167 on HC3. GPU training is not bitwise deterministic, and because the best epoch is selected by
validation F1, a small numerical difference can change which epoch is selected and cause a
materially different model to be loaded. When a model's validation curve is nearly flat across
epochs, as BERT's is, this effect is amplified. The BERT figures in Table 1 are accurate reports of
what those runs produced, and the saved probability arrays substantiate them, but they are not
exactly reproducible.

**The ensemble did not improve on the best single model.** On DAIGT the ensemble scored 0.9925
against DeBERTa's 0.9933, and on HC3 the two tied at 0.9992. This is a negative result and is
reported as such. Its mechanism is instructive. Soft voting benefits from members that are
comparably strong and that make different errors. The second condition holds here to an unusual
degree: on the HC3 test set BERT misclassified fifteen documents and DeBERTa misclassified one, and
there was no document that both models got wrong. A perfect combiner would therefore have achieved
a flawless result. The first condition fails badly, however, because BERT makes fifteen times as
many errors as DeBERTa. Any mixing weight large enough to let BERT correct DeBERTa's single error
is also large enough to admit BERT's fifteen errors. BERT's instability across seeds compounds the
problem.

The reported mixing weight of 0.00 on HC3 should not be read as a rejection of BERT. Every weight
from 0.00 to 0.50 produces identical validation F1 and identical test F1, an eleven-way tie, and the
implementation returns the first maximum it encounters. The correct interpretation is that the
optimum is a flat plateau which a 480-document validation set cannot resolve.

Alternative combination strategies were evaluated under the same protocol, selecting on validation
and reporting on test. Averaging three DeBERTa runs that differed only in random seed reached 0.9958
on DAIGT, and a logistic-regression meta-learner stacked over all five models reached 0.9950, both
exceeding the weighted soft vote of 0.9925. Equal-weight voting over all five models performed
worse than DeBERTa alone, at 0.9900 on DAIGT and 0.9783 on HC3, because it permits Naïve Bayes to
outvote the strongest member. The lesson is that when members differ greatly in strength, a learned
combiner is needed; simple averaging assumes a rough parity that does not hold here. These
alternatives are reported for completeness and were not adopted, since the ensemble row of the
specified table pairs the two models the project required.

### Limitations

The maximum sequence length of 128 tokens, fixed by the project specification, truncates 99.6
percent of DAIGT essays, and the median essay contributes only 31.3 percent of its tokens. The
DAIGT results should be read as classification from the opening portion of each essay rather than
from the whole document, and a longer sequence length would probably improve them.

Each configuration was trained once. Only the winning configuration per model and dataset was
repeated across three seeds, and the widest observed spread of 0.0267 F1 exceeds most of the
differences between configurations within Table 1. Rankings among the eight configurations of a
given model should therefore not be treated as established.

The two model families receive different text representations, cleaned text for the classical
models and raw text for the transformers. This is methodologically appropriate but means Table 2
compares complete pipelines rather than architectures in isolation.

The eight duplicated documents in the HC3 test set inflate all HC3 results very slightly, by at most
0.0010 F1 for any model. Some of these are artefacts of corpus collection rather than genuine
ChatGPT answers.

Finally, both datasets were reduced to six thousand documents to match the midterm protocol, so
absolute results would likely differ if the full corpora were used.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Fine-tuned transformers improved on the classical baselines established at midterm, and DeBERTa
proved the stronger of the two, reaching a weighted F1 of 0.9933 on DAIGT and 0.9992 on HC3 against
best classical results of 0.9875 and 0.9333. The improvement was modest where the classical baseline
was already near ceiling and substantial where it was not. Two findings run counter to expectation
and are reported rather than omitted: BERT's apparent advantage over the Support Vector Machine on
DAIGT does not survive a change of random seed, and the ensemble did not outperform its stronger
member because the two members differ too greatly in accuracy for probability averaging to help.

A detector that scores 99\% on a machine-generated-text benchmark may have modelled how machine language differs from human language, or how one corpus was punctuated and encoded, and the headline number does not distinguish them. We propose a decomposition that measures the difference. A surface-only model reads 47 orthographic features and never sees a word, a content-only model sees text after punctuation, casing and non-ASCII characters are stripped, and a full model is a fine-tuned transformer on raw text. Applied to DAIGT V2 and HC3 across five model families and eight configurations, the two corpora separate. On HC3 the surface-only and content-only arms are indistinguishable, 3.20\% against 3.26\% test error, so orthography alone matches content alone. On DAIGT V2 content is stronger by a factor of 7.9 in error, and a bag-of-words model on surface-normalised text comes within 0.0016 F1 of the best transformer, inside the measured seed range. Three controls change what the results mean. BERT emits identical token identifiers for strings differing only by the whitespace cue that dominates discussion of HC3, so it cannot represent that cue yet still reaches 0.9916 F1, making the cue sufficient in isolation but unnecessary in practice. A pipeline control establishes that the corresponding null is not a cleaning step that failed to run. A label-free control shows these models label random character strings as human in 100\% of cases at 0.98 confidence, so an adversarial collapse to 0.3644 F1 cannot be read as a targeted vulnerability. Surface form is informative on both corpora, and we claim only that it reaches parity with content on one.

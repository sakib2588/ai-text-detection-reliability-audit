\section{Method}

\subsection{Datasets}

DAIGT V2~\cite{Thedrcat2023daigtv2} contains 44,868 argumentative student essays, 27,371 human-written from the PERSUADE corpus and 17,497 machine-generated by a mixture of 2023-era systems including GPT-3.5, Llama-2, Mistral-7B and Falcon-180B, and was assembled for a public detection competition~\cite{Kaggle2023llmdetect}. The human side derives from the PERSUADE corpus of student argumentative writing~\cite{Crossley2023persuade}. HC3~\cite{Guo2023close} contains question-answer pairs from five English sources, contrasting human answers with responses from a single generator, GPT-3.5-Turbo-0301, over 85,449 rows~\cite{HelloSimpleAI2023hc3}. Both were class-balanced for training, giving 34,994 DAIGT V2 rows and 53,806 HC3 rows, and partitioned once into fixed 80/10/10 splits used unchanged by every model reported here. Training partitions are balanced to within 0.002 of parity, which matters for interpreting the degenerate-prediction control in Section~\ref{sec:confound}.

A duplicate-detection pass over the unbalanced corpora finds DAIGT V2 essentially free of internal duplication, six rows in six groups, 0.01\%, with no text appearing under both labels. HC3 carries 6,118 duplicate rows in 5,986 groups, 7.16\%, plus 476 rows labelled as machine-generated that are collection artefacts rather than model output, typically stored API failure messages. At the smaller scale where every leaked row can be inspected by hand, eight of 1,200 HC3 test rows, 0.67\%, also appear in training, and rescoring on the leakage-excluded subset moves F1 by at most 0.0010 without changing any ranking. We therefore report the contamination as a data-quality property rather than as an explanation of any accuracy figure.

\subsection{Models and the combinations evaluated}

Five model families are evaluated. Three are classical, Naive Bayes, logistic regression and a linear support vector machine, and each is run under two representations, bag-of-words and TF-IDF, giving six classical configurations per dataset. Earlier work on this corpus reported only the single winning representation per classical model at full scale. We report the complete six-cell grid so the representation choice is visible rather than assumed. Two are transformers, \texttt{bert-base-uncased} and \texttt{microsoft/deberta-v3-base}, fine-tuned over a grid of learning rate $\{2\mathrm{e}{-5}, 3\mathrm{e}{-5}\}$, batch size $\{16, 32\}$ and weight decay $\{0.01, 0.1\}$, sixteen configurations per dataset, with the operating point selected on validation F1.

Classical models receive text through the normalisation pipeline described below. Transformers receive raw text truncated to 128 tokens, a constraint inherited from the study this corpus was built for, whose consequences we quantify in Section~\ref{sec:limits} rather than leave implicit.

\subsection{The surface-content decomposition}

The central instrument of this paper separates how much of a benchmark's human-versus-machine separability is available from surface form alone and how much from content alone. Three arms are compared on identical splits.

The \emph{surface-only} arm uses 47 orthographic features and never reads word identity. These cover punctuation character rates, whitespace behaviour including the count of spaces preceding punctuation, casing statistics, document and sentence length, non-ASCII and emoji rates, and digit rates. Because no lexical item enters the feature vector, this arm cannot access content by construction.

The \emph{content-only} arm applies the surface normalisation used by the classical pipeline, lowercasing and replacing every character outside \texttt{[a-z\textbackslash s]} with a space, then removing stopwords and lemmatising, before bag-of-words vectorisation. Punctuation, casing and non-ASCII characters cannot survive this transformation, so the arm cannot access the surface cues the first arm isolates.

The \emph{full} arm is the fine-tuned transformer on raw text. Surface-only and content-only share one classifier family, logistic regression, so they are directly comparable to each other. The transformer differs in family and is reported as a reference point rather than as a matched arm, and we draw no conclusion from a transformer-to-classical difference alone.

\subsection{Statistics and confound checks}
\label{sec:confound}

Every reported figure comes from a committed script writing JSON, and every transformer baseline is read from the deployed checkpoint's own record rather than from the maximum over its hyperparameter grid. These are not stylistic preferences. An earlier version of this analysis compared a grid maximum against a single fixed configuration and reported differences that did not exist, which is why baselines are now checkpoint-matched throughout.

We report error rate alongside F1 because at this accuracy ceiling F1 differences compress, and macro alongside weighted F1 so that no result can be produced by class skew. Seed spread over three seeds is reported as a range, and we treat it as an order-of-magnitude indication rather than a test statistic, since fine-tuning outcomes are known to vary materially with initialisation and data order~\cite{Dodge2020fine,Mosbach2020stability}. We do not run parametric tests at $n \le 30$.

Three controls guard the specific claims made below. First, a \emph{pipeline-fires} control. Our whitespace cleaning produces bit-identical predictions for BERT on HC3, which alone would be indistinguishable from cleaning that silently failed to run. The same pipeline applied to DAIGT V2 does change BERT's predictions, since emoji removal is visible to its tokeniser, establishing that the pipeline fires and that the null is specific to whitespace. Second, a \emph{tokenisation} control, comparing token identifiers for minimally different strings to establish what each tokeniser can represent. Third, a \emph{label-free} control, scoring inputs that carry no human-versus-machine information at all, including uniform random character strings, token-shuffled text, punctuation-only strings and the empty string. Without this last control a confident prediction on corrupted input is indistinguishable from a learned response to it.

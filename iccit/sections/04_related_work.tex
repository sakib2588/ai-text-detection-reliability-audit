\section{Related Work}

Across the broader literature on both datasets, essentially every study evaluating purely in-distribution reports F1 in the 97-100\% range~\cite{Guo2023close,Su2023plus,Zhou2024exploiting,Ansary2026multirepresentation,Lamsiyah2025daigt}, a saturation meaning another in-distribution accuracy number does not by itself constitute a novel contribution. A separate thread shows this apparent solvedness is fragile. Antoun et al.~\cite{Antoun2023towards} attack HC3-trained detectors with misspellings and homoglyphs, a detector scoring 99.88 F1 in-domain falls to 33.57\% raw accuracy on a hand-written adversarial set. Su et al.~\cite{Su2023plus} show reframing detection from question-answering to semantic-invariant tasks drops accuracy from 99.82\% to roughly 91.7\%. Borile and Abrate~\cite{Borile2025generalize} find that ablating around twenty feed-forward neurons, about 0.05\% of a BERT-based detector, improves out-of-distribution accuracy by up to 6.9 points, mechanistic evidence that a measurable share of in-distribution performance is carried by dataset-specific, non-generalizing features. A third thread documents concrete surface artifacts, Tian et al.~\cite{Tian2023multiscale} discover an extra space before punctuation in HC3-English human answers absent from ChatGPT answers, Baidya et al.~\cite{Baidya2026detecting} independently document a length confound in the same dataset, and Ardeshirifar~\cite{Ardeshirifar2025comparing} standardizes punctuation and contractions for the same underlying reason, three independent groups converging on the conclusion that reported HC3 accuracy is partly a function of collection artifacts rather than language understanding. Park et al.~\cite{Park2024investigating} extend this further, showing HC3-trained detectors latch onto prompt-specific collection shortcuts and constructing adversarial instructions that exploit them directly. On the classical-baseline side, Kubrusly et al.~\cite{Kubrusly2025detecting} report strong bag-of-words and TF-IDF results on HC3 without any transformer, the natural comparison point for the classical models in our own pipeline.

The work closest to ours is Alikhanov et al.~\cite{Alikhanov2026generated}, who merge HC3 and DAIGT V2 into one 124,195-sample corpus and evaluate under a topic-held-out split, reporting substantially lower accuracy (82.87-88.86\%) than in-distribution numbers reported elsewhere. Their design asks whether one model can handle both datasets at once, whereas our strict one-way cross-dataset transfer asks whether detection knowledge acquired on one corpus carries to a genuinely disjoint one, and our artifact-cleaning and adversarial-robustness results decompose \emph{why} transfer fails rather than only measuring that it does.
